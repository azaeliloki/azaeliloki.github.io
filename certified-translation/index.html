Each certified copy carries my wet-ink signature and the official stamp of a translator accredited by the Cour d’appel de Lomé — usually on every page, with a short attestation that the translation is true to the original. Legalisation

Who walks the file through, and what happens to your original

Togo is not a party to the Hague Apostille Convention: legalisation runs through the town hall (mairie) where I am registered, then the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, then the consulate of the destination country. I walk the file through myself, counter by counter. You do not need anyone in Lomé.

  • Where to send the original. Legalisation applies to the original document, not a copy: it has to reach Lomé physically, addressed to me. The receiving address and the carrier are given with your quote — do not ship anything before you have them.
  • What happens to it in transit. The original stays in my possession from lodging to collection. It is never handed to an intermediary, and I confirm each stage as it clears.
  • A full scan is taken before anything is lodged. That scan is what allows the file to be reconstituted if the document goes astray.
  • If the document is lost or damaged. A Togolese civil-status record can be re-issued by the town hall that issued it: I file the re-issue application and follow it through. The extent of my liability is the one set out in the terms of service.
  • What comes back to you. The legalised original and its sworn translation, stamped and bound together, shipped to the address of your choice — plus the secure PDF on completion.

Fees. Stamps, official charges and carriage are re-invoiced at cost and stated in your quote. If you would rather settle with the administrations directly, you can: I carry the file, you keep the fees in your own hands.

Turnaround depends on the destination consulate, and I will not quote it from guesswork: tell me the country and the authority involved, I check with the legalisation service and confirm it with your quote — together with the answer on whether the original is required.

Comparison

Certified, sworn, notarised or legalised — what’s the difference?

English-speaking buyers are asked for four different things and the words are used loosely. In Togo, a sworn (assermentée) translation is signed and stamped by a translator accredited by the Cour d’appel de Lomé — and that is what embassies, universities and courts mean when they ask for a “certified” or an “official” translation. A notarised copy is a separate act and does not replace the sworn stamp. Legalisation is a further step again, applied to the file as a whole — the translation and the original travel together.
