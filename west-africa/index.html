Certified FR↔EN Translation for Anglophone West Africa

West Africa runs on two languages. Documents have to cross.

A contract drafted in Accra is signed in Lomé. A Lagos judgment is produced before a Togolese court. A Dakar tender is filed in French. Every one of those crossings needs a sworn translation — and no agency in London or Toronto can supply one, however good its linguists.

The Principle

Why the seal has to be local

A document has no legal effect before a Togolese, Ivorian, Beninese or Senegalese authority unless it carries the stamp of a translator sworn before a court inside that system. This is not a quality judgement about other translators. It is a jurisdictional fact: the receiving court, ministry or notary recognises the register it can check, and that register is national.

I am sworn before the Cour d’appel de Lomé, in French and English, in both directions. For any file that has to be produced before an authority in Togo — or that leaves Togo for an English-speaking one — my stamp is what gives it standing. Acceptance always remains the receiving authority’s decision; tell me the destination and I will match the format it requires.

Corridors

What crosses the language border

Counterparties and courts in Ghana, Nigeria, The Gambia, Sierra Leone and Liberia work in English. Togo, Benin, Côte d’Ivoire, Burkina Faso, Mali, Niger and Senegal work in French. Trade, litigation and family matters cross that line constantly, and each crossing produces the same handful of documents.
